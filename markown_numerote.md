115: # 1 Introduction
116: 
117: The Lebesgue integral is a generalization of the Riemann integral, which students usually encounter in high school and during the first years of undergraduate studies. While the Riemann integral gives a meaning to the area under a curve, it quickly shows its limitations when we want to integrate more general functions, such as those with too many discontinuities.
118: 
119: At the beginning of the 20th century, Henri Lebesgue developed a new way of approaching integration, based not on subdivisions of the x-axis (as in Riemann's method), but on the measure of the sets of values taken by a function. This new approach allows the integration of a much larger class of functions and possesses powerful convergence properties.
120: 
121: The goal of this chapter is to introduce this new integral step-by-step, relying on intuition, simple examples, and the main ideas that form the foundation of modern analysis.
122: 
123: # 2 Motivations and limitations of the Riemann integral
124: 
125: ## 2.1 Recall of the Riemann integral
126: 
127: The Riemann integral is based on the following idea: to compute the area under a curve f defined on an interval [a, b], we divide this interval into small subintervals, then sum the areas of rectangles approximating the curve. If this sum converges as the subdivisions get finer, we say that f is Riemann integrable.
128: 
129: ![img-1.jpeg](img-1.jpeg)
130: 
131: Figure 1: Example of a function to integrate over [a, b].
132: 
133: Lebesgue Integration
134: 
135: 5
136: 
137: ![img-2.jpeg](img-2.jpeg)
138: 
139: Figure 2: Left Riemann sums $$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} f(a + k\Delta x)\Delta x$$: as the subdivision is refined ($$\Delta x \to 0$$), the sum of the rectangle areas converges to $$\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx$$, if $$f$$ is Riemann integrable.
140: 
141: However, this method relies heavily on the continuity of the function or, at least, on the “smallness” of its discontinuities.
142: 
143: ## 2.2 A famous limitation: the indicator function of rationals
144: 
145: Consider the function defined on $$[0, 1]$$:
146: 
147: $$f(x) = \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]}(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x \in \mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1], \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$
148: 
149: It is the indicator function of the rational numbers in $$[0, 1]$$. It takes the value 1 on rationals, and 0 on irrationals.
150: 
151: - It is discontinuous everywhere.
152: - Every interval contains infinitely many rationals and irrationals.
153: 
154: This function is not Riemann integrable, because its lower and upper integrals are:
155: 
156: $$\int_{0}^{1} f(x) \, dx = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \overline{\int_{0}^{1}} f(x) \, dx = 1.$$
157: 
158: Thus, the Riemann integral cannot exist in this case.
159: 
160: ## 2.3 Another limitation: passing to the limit in a sequence of functions
161: 
162: Consider the sequence $$(f_n)$$ defined by:
163: 
164: $$f_n(x) = \begin{cases} n & \text{if } 0 \le x \le \frac{1}{n}, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$
165: 
166: Lebesgue Integration
167: 
168: 6
169: 
170: Each $f_n$ is Riemann integrable on $[0, 1]$, and we have:
171: 
172: $$\int_0^1 f_n(x) \, dx = 1 \quad \text{for all } n.$$
173: 
174: But when $n \to \infty$, $f_n(x) \to 0$ for all $x > 0$, and $f_n(0) = n \to \infty$, so:
175: 
176: $$f_n(x) \longrightarrow 0 \quad \text{for all } x \in (0, 1].$$
177: 
178: We might expect that the limit of the integral equals the integral of the limit, i.e., 0. But here, this is not the case: the interchange of limit and integral fails.
179: 
180: The Lebesgue integral allows, under simple conditions, to justify this type of interchange.
181: 
182: ## 2.4 Towards a new approach
183: 
184: These examples show that the Riemann integral, while very intuitive, has limitations. It is not suited for highly discontinuous functions, nor for certain limit operations (like sequences of functions).
185: 
186: The Lebesgue integral will allow us:
187: 
188: to integrate very "irregular" functions;
189: to easily interchange limits and integrals (under certain conditions);
190: to handle cases impossible for Riemann's method.
191: 
192: We will therefore start with some fundamental tools: measure theory and measurable functions.
193: 
194: ## 3 Measure and $\sigma$-Algebras
195: 
196: Before defining the Lebesgue integral, we need to understand how to “measure” sets in a rigorous way. This is the purpose of measure theory.
197: 
198: ### 3.1 $\sigma$-Algebras
199: 
200: Definition 3.1. Let $X$ be a non-empty set. A $\sigma$-algebra $\mathscr{A}$ on $X$ is a collection of subsets of $X$ such that:
201: 
202: 1. \(X\in \mathcal{A}\)
203: 2. If \(A \in \mathcal{A}\), then \(A^c = X \setminus A \in \mathcal{A}\);
204: 3. If \((A_{n})_{n\in \mathbb{N}}\subset \mathcal{A}\), then \(\bigcup_{n = 0}^{\infty}A_n\in \mathcal{A}\)
205: 
206: In other words, a $\sigma$-algebra is closed under complements and countable unions. It is therefore also closed under countable intersections.
207: 
208: Lebesgue Integration
209: 
210: 7
211: 
212: Example 3.2. On \( X = \{1,2\} \), the following collection is a \( \sigma \)-algebra:
213: 
214: \[
215: \mathscr {A} = \{\emptyset , \{1 \}, \{2 \}, \{1, 2 \} \}.
216: \]
217: 
218: On \(\mathbb{R}\), the most important example is the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra, generated by open intervals \((a,b)\). It contains all standard intervals, countable sets, etc.
219: 
220: Definition 3.3 (Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\mathbb{R}^n\)). The Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\mathbb{R}^n\), denoted \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)\), is the \(\sigma\)-algebra generated by the open sets of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (for the usual topology). It contains all open and closed sets, intervals, and is stable under countable unions, countable intersections, and complements.
221: 
222: Definition 3.4 (Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra on an open set \(\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n\)). Let \(\Omega\) be an open set in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). The Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra of \(\Omega\), denoted \(\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\), is the \(\sigma\)-algebra induced by \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)\) on \(\Omega\), that is:
223: 
224: \[
225: \mathscr {B} (\Omega) = \{A \cap \Omega \mid A \in \mathscr {B} (\mathbb {R} ^ {n}) \}.
226: \]
227: 
228: In other words, it contains intersections of Borel sets of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) with \(\Omega\).
229: 
230: ### 3.2 Measures
231: 
232: Definition 3.5. Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be a \(\sigma\)-algebra on a set \(X\). A mapping \(\mu : \mathcal{A} \to [0, +\infty]\) is called a measure if:
233: 
234: 1.  \( \mu(\emptyset)=0; \)
235: 
236: 2. \(\mu\) is \(\sigma\)-additive: for every sequence \((A_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}\) of disjoint sets in \(\mathcal{A}\), we have:
237: 
238: \[
239: \mu \left(\bigcup_ {n = 0} ^ {\infty} A _ {n}\right) = \sum_ {n = 0} ^ {\infty} \mu (A _ {n}).
240: \]
241: 
242: Example 3.6 (Dirac Measure). Let \( X \) be a set and \( x_0 \in X \) a fixed point. The Dirac measure at \( x_0 \), denoted \( \delta_{x_0} \), on \( (X, \mathcal{P}(X)) \) is defined by:
243: 
244: \[
245: \delta_ {x _ {0}} (A) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text {   if   } x _ {0} \in A, \\ 0 & \text {   if   } x _ {0} \notin A, \end{array} \right. \quad \text {   for   all   } A \subset X \text {(i.e.,   for   all   } A \in \mathcal {P} (X)).
246: \]
247: 
248: Why \(\delta_{x_0}\) is a positive measure.
249: 
250: - \(\delta_{x_0}(\emptyset) = 0\) since \(x_0 \notin \emptyset\).
251: - Let \((A_{n})_{n\in \mathbb{N}}\) be a sequence of pairwise disjoint sets. Then:
252: 
253: \[
254: \delta_ {x _ {0}} \left(\bigcup_ {n = 0} ^ {\infty} A _ {n}\right) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text { if } x _ {0} \in \bigcup_ {n = 0} ^ {\infty} A _ {n}, \\ 0 & \text { otherwise }. \end{array} \right.
255: \]
256: 
257: But \( x_0 \in \bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty} A_n \) if and only if there exists a unique \( n_0 \) such that \( x_0 \in A_{n_0} \) (since the \( A_n \) are disjoint). Therefore:
258: 
259: \[
260: \sum_ {n = 0} ^ {\infty} \delta_ {x _ {0}} (A _ {n}) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text { if } x _ {0} \in A _ {n _ {0}} \text { for   a   unique } n _ {0}, \\ 0 & \text { otherwise }. \end{array} \right.
261: \]
262: 
263: Hence:
264: 
265: \[
266: \delta_ {x _ {0}} \left(\bigcup_ {n = 0} ^ {\infty} A _ {n}\right) = \sum_ {n = 0} ^ {\infty} \delta_ {x _ {0}} (A _ {n}).
267: \]
268: 
269: Lebesgue Integration
270: 
271: 8
272: 
273: Thus, $\delta_{x_0}$ is a **positive measure**.
274: 
275: **Example 3.7** (Counting Measure). *Let $X$ be any set. The **counting measure** $\mu$ on $(X, \mathscr{P}(X))$ is defined by:*
276: 
277: $$\mu(A) = \begin{cases} \text{the number of elements of } A & \text{if } A \text{ is finite,} \\ +\infty & \text{if } A \text{ is infinite,} \end{cases} \quad \text{for all } A \in \mathscr{P}(X).$$
278: 
279: *Why $\mu$ is a positive measure.*
280: 
281: - $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$.
282: - Let $(A_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ be a family of pairwise disjoint sets. Then:
283: 
284: $$\mu \left( \bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty} A_n \right) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \mu(A_n),$$
285: 
286: because the elements of $\bigcup A_n$ are all distinct and each belongs to exactly one $A_n$. Thus, we simply count the elements in each $A_n$ and sum them.
287: 
288: Therefore, $\mu$ is indeed a **positive measure on** $(X, \mathscr{P}(X))$.
289: 
290: **Example 3.8** (Intuitive approach to length: Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$). *The **Lebesgue measure** on $\mathbb{R}$*, denoted $\lambda$, is the unique measure such that:*
291: 
292: - It assigns to each interval $[a, b] \subset \mathbb{R}$ its length:
293: 
294: $$\lambda([a, b]) = b - a.$$
295: 
296: - It is translation-invariant: for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, $\lambda(A + x) = \lambda(A)$.
297: 
298: It extends to a well-defined measure on the Borel $\sigma$-algebra of $\mathbb{R}$ (and then to a larger $\sigma$-algebra called the Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra).
299: 
300: **More generally: Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^N$.**
301: 
302: **Example 3.9** (Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^N$ – intuitive approach). *The **Lebesgue measure** on $\mathbb{R}^N$ generalizes the notions of length (in 1D, see Example 3.8), area (in 2D), or volume (in 3D and higher) to much more general sets than just boxes or regular domains.*
303: 
304: Intuitively, the Lebesgue measure $\lambda^N$ of a set $A \subset \mathbb{R}^N$ represents its geometric “size,” even if $A$ is highly irregular.
305: 
306: It is constructed so that:
307: 
308: - The measure of a box $I = \prod_{i=1}^N [a_i, b_i]$ is the product of its side lengths: $\lambda^N(I) = \prod_{i=1}^N (b_i - a_i)$.
309: - It is additive on disjoint sets: the measure of a disjoint union is the sum of the measures.
310: - It is translation-invariant: for all $x \in \mathbb{R}^N$, $\lambda^N(A + x) = \lambda^N(A)$.
311: 
312: **Definition 3.10.** *A **measurable space** is a pair $(X, \mathscr{A})$ where:*
313: 
314: - $X$ is a set,
315: - $\mathscr{A}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra on $X$.
316:

321: ### 3.3 Measured Spaces
322: 
323: Definition 3.11. A measured space is a triple $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ where:
324: 
325: - $X$ is a set,
326: - $\mathcal{A}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra on $X$,
327: - $\mu$ is a measure on $\mathcal{A}$.
328: 
329: This is the framework in which we can define measurable functions and then integrate them.
330: 
331: Remark 3.12. The set of functions we can integrate depends on the chosen measure. The Lebesgue integral is therefore defined with respect to a measure.
332: 
333: ### 3.4 Key ideas to remember
334: 
335: - A $\sigma$-algebra specifies the “measurable” sets (compatible with the measure).
336: - A measure gives meaning to the “size” or “volume” of these sets.
337: - Lebesgue measure is the most common in real analysis.
338: 
339: We will now define what a measurable function is: in other words, a function well adapted to the measure, which we can integrate.
340: 
341: ## 4 Measurable Functions
342: 
343: The Lebesgue integral does not apply to all functions, but only to those that are well adapted to the measure defined on the domain. These functions are called measurable functions.
344: 
345: ### 4.1 Definition
346: 
347: Definition 4.1. Let $(X, \mathcal{A})$ be a measurable space (i.e., $X$ equipped with a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{A}$), and let $f: X \to \mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty, -\infty\} = \overline{\mathbb{R}}$ be a function.
348: 
349: We say that $f$ is measurable (with respect to $\mathcal{A}$) if for every real number $a \in \mathbb{R}$, the set
350: 
351: $$\{x \in X \mid f(x) > a\}$$
352: 
353: belongs to $\mathcal{A}$.
354: 
355: Remark 4.2. We could replace the condition "$f(x) > a$" by "$f(x) \geq a$", "$f(x) < a$", or "$f(x) \leq a$": these are equivalent for defining measurability.

361: ### 4.2 Examples of measurable functions
362: 
363: Example 4.3. Any continuous function \( f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \) is measurable (with respect to the Borel \( \sigma \)-algebra).
364: 
365: Solution. For a continuous \( f \), the set \( f^{-1}(]a, +\infty[) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid f(x) > a\} \) is open for all \( a \in \mathbb{R} \) and hence Borel measurable.
366: 
367: Example 4.4. The indicator function \(\mathbf{1}_A\) of a measurable set \(A\in \mathcal{A}\), defined by:
368: 
369: \[
370: \mathbf {1} _ {A} (x) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text { if } x \in A, \\ 0 & \text { otherwise }, \end{array} \right.
371: \]
372: 
373: is measurable.
374: 
375: Solution. If \(a < 0\), then \(\{x \mid \mathbf{1}_A(x) > a\} = \mathbb{R}\), which is measurable.
376: 
377: - If \(0 \leq a < 1\), then \(\{x \mid \mathbf{1}_A(x) > a\} = A\), which is measurable by assumption.
378: - If \(a \geq 1\), then \(\{x \mid \mathbf{1}_A(x) > a\} = \emptyset\), which is measurable.
379: 
380: In all cases, the preimage set \(\{x\mid \mathbf{1}_A(x) > a\}\) is measurable. Therefore, \(\mathbf{1}_A\) is measurable.
381: 
382: □

392: ### 4.3 Stability of measurable functions
393: 
394: Measurable functions are stable under many usual operations:
395: 
396: Theorem 4.6 (Stability under Composition). Let \((X, \mathcal{A})\), \((Y, \mathcal{B})\), and \((Z, \mathcal{C})\) be three measurable spaces. If \(f: X \to Y\) is \(\mathcal{A} / \mathcal{B}\)-measurable and \(g: Y \to Z\) is \(\mathcal{B} / \mathcal{C}\)-measurable, then the composition \(g \circ f: X \to Z\) is \(\mathcal{A} / \mathcal{C}\)-measurable.
397: 
398: Proof. Let \( C \in \mathcal{C} \). Since \( g \) is \( \mathcal{B} / \mathcal{C} \)-measurable, we have \( g^{-1}(C) \in \mathcal{B} \). Because \( f \) is \( \mathcal{A} / \mathcal{B} \)-measurable, the preimage of any set in \( \mathcal{B} \) under \( f \) belongs to \( \mathcal{A} \), hence \( f^{-1}(g^{-1}(C)) \in \mathcal{A} \). By the elementary property of preimages,
399: 
400: \[
401: (g \circ f) ^ {- 1} (C) = f ^ {- 1} \bigl (g ^ {- 1} (C) \bigr).
402: \]
403: 
404: Thus, \((g\circ f)^{-1}(C)\in \mathcal{A}\) for every \(C\in \mathcal{C}\), which proves that \(g\circ f\) is \(\mathcal{A} / \mathcal{C}\)-measurable.
405: 
406: Property 4.7 (Stability under elementary operations). Let \( f \) and \( g \) be two measurable functions from \( X \) to \( \mathbb{R} \). Then:
407: 
408: 1. \(f + g\) is measurable.
409: 2. \(f \cdot g\) is measurable.

417: 
418: Proof. The key fact in all these proofs is: if $f$ is measurable, then for all $a \in \mathbb{R}$, the set $\{x \in X \mid f(x) > a\}$ is measurable and conversely.
419: 
420: 1. Measurability of $f + g$:
421: 
422: Let $a \in \mathbb{R}$. We want to show:
423: 
424: $$\{x \mid f(x) + g(x) > a\} \in \mathscr{A}.$$
425: 
426: We can write:
427: 
428: $$\{x \mid f(x) + g(x) > a\} = \bigcup_{q \in \mathbb{Q}} (\{x \mid f(x) > q\} \cap \{x \mid g(x) > a - q\}).$$

Intersections of measurable sets are measurable, and countable unions of measurable sets are measurable. Therefore, $f + g$ is measurable.
431: 
432: 2. Measurability of $f \cdot g$:
433:

435:

441: 
442: $$\{x \in X : h(x) < a\} \in \mathcal{A}.$$
443: 
444: - If $a \le 0$, then
445:

451:

453:

468: Theorem 4.8 (Stability of limit operations; complete space). Let \((X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)\) be a measure space. Let \((f_n)_{n \geq 1}\) be a sequence of \(\mathcal{A}\)-measurable functions taking values in \(\overline{\mathbb{R}}\). Then the functions
469: 
470: \[
471: \sup _ {n \geq 1} f _ {n}, \quad \inf _ {n \geq 1} f _ {n}, \quad \operatorname * {l i m s u p} _ {n \to \infty} f _ {n}, \quad \operatorname * {l i m i n f} _ {n \to \infty} f _ {n}
472: \]
473: 
474: are \(\mathcal{A}\)-measurable. In particular, if \(f_n \to f\) \(\mu\)-almost everywhere, then \(f\) is measurable (here it is necessary to assume that \((X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)\) is complete, i.e. such that if \(N \in \mathcal{A}\) and \(\mu(N) = 0\), then every subset \(B \subset N\) also belongs to \(\mathcal{A}\). 475: 
476: Proof. We use the classical criterion: a function \( g: X \to \overline{\mathbb{R}} \) is measurable if and only if \( \{g < a\} \in \mathcal{A} \) for every \( a \in \mathbb{R} \).
477: 
478: (1) Supremum. For \(a \in \mathbb{R}\),
479: 
480: \[
481: \left\{\sup _ {n \geq 1} f _ {n} <   a \right\} = \bigcap_ {n = 1} ^ {\infty} \left\{f _ {n} <   a \right\}.
482: \]
483: 
484: Since each \(\{f_n < a\} \in \mathcal{A}\) and \(\mathcal{A}\) is stable under countable intersections, we obtain the measurability of \(\sup_n f_n\).
485: 
486: (2) Infimum. Similarly,
487: 
488: \[
489: \left\{\inf _ {n \geq 1} f _ {n} > a \right\} = \bigcap_ {n = 1} ^ {\infty} \left\{f _ {n} > a \right\},
490: \]
491: 
492: hence \(\inf_n f_n\) is measurable (or equivalently via \(-\inf f_n = \sup(-f_n)\)).
493: 
494: (3) Limsup. For \(a \in \mathbb{R}\),
495: 
496: \[
497: \left\{\limsup _ {n \to \infty} f _ {n} <   a \right\} = \bigcup_ {k = 1} ^ {\infty} \bigcap_ {n \geq k} \{f _ {n} <   a \}.
498: \]
499: 
500: The stability of \(\mathcal{A}\) under countable unions and intersections implies the measurability of \(\limsup f_n\).
501: 
502: (4) Liminf. Similarly,
503: 
504: \[
505: \left\{\liminf _ {n \to \infty} f _ {n} > a \right\} = \bigcup_ {k = 1} ^ {\infty} \bigcap_ {n \geq k} \{f _ {n} > a \},
506: \]
507: 
508: which shows that \(\liminf f_n\) is measurable.
509: 
510: (5) Almost everywhere limit. Assume that \( f_{n} \to f \) \( \mu \)-almost everywhere. Then on \( E := \{x \in X : \lim f_{n}(x) \text{ exists}\} \) (with \( \mu(X \setminus E) = 0 \)), we have \( f = \limsup f_{n} = \liminf f_{n} \). Let \( g := \limsup f_{n} \), which is measurable by (3). We have \( f = g \) on \( E \), hence for every \( a \in \mathbb{R} \),
511: 
512: \[
513: \{f <   a \} \Delta \{g <   a \} \subset X \setminus E,
514: \]
515: 
516: where  \( \Delta \)  denotes the symmetric difference. Since  \( X \setminus E \)  is measurable with measure zero and the space is complete, every subset of  \( X \setminus E \)  is measurable; thus  \( \{f < a\} \in A \)  for all a, and f is measurable. ☐

522: ### 4.4 Negligible sets and "almost everywhere" property
523: 
524: Definition 4.9 (Negligible set). Let \((X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)\) be a measure space. A set \(N \subset X\) is said to be negligible (or null set) for the measure \(\mu\) if:
525: 
526: \[
527: \mu (N) = 0.
528: \]
529: 
530: In other words, \(N\) belongs to \(\mathcal{A}\) and has measure zero.
531: 
532: Examples 4.10 (Examples for the Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) on \(\mathbb{R}\)).
533: 
534: - Example 1: a singleton is negligible.
535: 
536: Let \(x_0 \in \mathbb{R}\). Then:
537: 
538: \[
539: \lambda (\{x _ {0} \}) = 0.
540: \]
541: 
542: Proof. For any \(\varepsilon > 0\), we can cover \(\{x_0\}\) with an interval \(I_{\varepsilon} = (x_0 - \varepsilon, x_0 + \varepsilon)\) whose measure is \(2\varepsilon\). By definition of Lebesgue measure as the infimum of lengths of open coverings:
543: 
544: \[
545: \lambda (\{x _ {0} \}) \leq 2 \varepsilon , \quad \forall \varepsilon > 0.
546: \]
547: 
548: Hence \(\lambda (\{x_0\}) = 0\)
549: 
550: - Example 2: a finite set is negligible.
551: 
552: Let \( A = \{x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n}\} \subset \mathbb{R} \). By finite additivity of Lebesgue measure:
553: 
554: \[
555: \lambda (A) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \lambda (\{x _ {i} \}) = 0.
556: \]
557: 
558: - Example 3: the set of rationals \(\mathbb{Q}\) is negligible.
559: 
560: Proof. The rationals are countable: \(\mathbb{Q} = \{q_1, q_2, q_3, \ldots\}\). Then:
561: 
562: \[
563: \lambda (\mathbb {Q}) = \lambda \left(\bigcup_ {n = 1} ^ {\infty} \{q _ {n} \}\right) \leq \sum_ {n = 1} ^ {\infty} \lambda (\{q _ {n} \}) = 0.
564: \]
565: 
566: By \(\sigma\)-additivity, \(\lambda(\mathbb{Q}) = 0\).
567: 
568: - Example 4: the Cantor set is negligible.
569: 
570: It is known that the Cantor set \(\mathcal{C} \subset [0,1]\) is uncountable, closed, and has no intervals, but:
571: 
572: \[
573: \lambda (\mathcal {C}) = 0.
574: \]
575: 
576: (The proof relies on the construction of the Cantor set by successive removal of intervals, whose total removed length equals 1.)

579: 
580: \[
581: \mu \left(\{x \in X \mid P (x) i s f a l s e \}\right) = 0.
582: \]
583: 
584: We also write: \( P(x) \) is true for \( \mu \)-almost every \( x \in X \), or simply \( P(x) \) is true a.e.

587:

590: Example 4.12. Let $f, g : X \to \mathbb{R}$ be two measurable functions. We say that $f = g$ almost everywhere on $X$ if:
591: 
592: $$\mu(\{x \in X \mid f(x) \neq g(x)\}) = 0.$$
593: 
594: That is, $f(x) = g(x)$ for $\mu$-almost every $x \in X$.

598: Proposition 4.13. Let $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ and let $N \subset \mathbb{R}$ be a negligible set (i.e. $\lambda(N) = 0$) such that $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R} \setminus N$. Then $f$ is (Lebesgue-)measurable.
599: 
600: Proof. Fix $a \in \mathbb{R}$. We decompose:
601: 
602: $$E_a = (\mathbb{R} \setminus N) \cap \{x : f(x) > a\} \cup N \cap \{x : f(x) > a\}.$$
603: 
604: On $\mathbb{R} \setminus N$, the function $f$ is continuous. Therefore:
605: 
606: $$\{x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus N : f(x) > a\} = (f|_{\mathbb{R} \setminus N})^{-1}((a, \infty))$$
607: 
608: is open in $\mathbb{R} \setminus N$, hence Borel measurable in $\mathbb{R}$ and thus Lebesgue-measurable. Moreover:
609: 
610: $$N \cap \{x : f(x) > a\} \subset N,$$
611: 
612: so it is negligible (any subset of a negligible set is measurable).
613: 
614: Thus, $E_a$ is the union of two measurable sets, hence measurable. As this holds for all $a \in \mathbb{R}$, $f$ is (Lebesgue-)measurable.

616: ### 4.5 Why is measurability important?
617: 
618: The measurability condition is what ensures that the sets on which the function “takes certain values” are measurable, and therefore integrable in the sense of Lebesgue.

642: ## 6 Integral of simple functions
643: 
644: To build the Lebesgue integral, we start by integrating the simplest possible functions: simple functions, i.e., those taking only finitely many values.
645: 
646: ### 6.1 Definition
647: 
648: Definition 6.1. Let $(X, \mathscr{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space. A simple (or step) function is any function $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$ that is measurable and takes only finitely many real values.
649: 
650: In other words, there exists an integer $n \in \mathbb{N}^*$, real numbers $a_1, \ldots, a_n \in \mathbb{R}$, and measurable sets $A_1, \ldots, A_n \in \mathscr{A}$ forming a partition of $X$ (with $f$ constant on each $A_i$), such that:
651: 
652: $$f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i \, \mathbf{1}_{A_i}(x), \quad \forall x \in X.$$
653: 
654: Each $A_i$ is called the level set associated with the value $a_i$. We denote by $\mathscr{E}$ the set of all simple functions.
655: 
656: Reminder. Let $A \subset X$. A finite family of sets $(A_1, \ldots, A_n) \subset \mathscr{A}$ is called a partition of $A$ if:
657: 
658: (i) $\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i = A,$
659: 
660: (ii) $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ for all $i \neq j$.
661: 
662: That is, the $A_i$ are pairwise disjoint and cover the whole set $A$.
663: 
664: Lebesgue Integration
665: 
666: 16
667: 
668: ### 6.2 Definition of the integral of a simple function
669: 
670: Definition 6.2. Let \( f = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \mathbf{1}_{A_i} \) be a non-negative simple function, with \( a_i \in \mathbb{R}^+ \), i.e. \( f \in \mathcal{E}^+ \), \( A_i \in \mathcal{A} \). We define the integral of \( f \) over \( X \) (or over a measurable subset \( E \subset X \)) by:
671: 
672: \[
673: \int_ {E} f d \mu = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} a _ {i} \mu (A _ {i} \cap E).
674: \]
675: 
676: Remark 6.3. This formula can be seen as a generalization of rectangle areas: for each value \( a_i \), we measure "how much" of \( E \) takes this value via \( \mu(A_i \cap E) \), then take a weighted sum.
677: 
678: ### 6.3 Simple example
679: 
680: Example 6.4. Let \( X = [0,2] \), equipped with the Lebesgue measure \( \lambda \), and consider the function:
681: 
682: \[
683: f (x) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \text { if } x \in [ 0, 1), \\ 2 & \text { if } x \in [ 1, 2 ]. \end{array} \right.
684: \]
685: 
686: We can write:
687: 
688: \[
689: f (x) = \mathbf {1} _ {[ 0, 1)} (x) + 2 \mathbf {1} _ {[ 1, 2 ]} (x),
690: \]
691: 
692: and hence:
693: 
694: \[
695: \int_ {[ 0, 2 ]} f (x) d \lambda (x) = 1 \lambda ([ 0, 1)) + 2 \lambda ([ 1, 2 ]) = 1 \times 1 + 2 \times 1 = 3.
696: \]

698: ### 6.4 Properties of the integral of simple functions
699: 
700: Property 6.5 (Linearity). Let \( f \) and \( g \) be two simple functions in \( \mathcal{E}^+ \), and \( \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R} \). Then:
701: 
702: \[
703: \int_ {E} (\alpha f + \beta g) d \mu = \alpha \int_ {E} f d \mu + \beta \int_ {E} g d \mu .
704: \]
705: 
706: Proof. Since \( f \) and \( g \) are simple functions, there exist measurable partitions of \( E \) such that:
707: 
708: \[
709: f = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} a _ {i} \mathbf {1} _ {A _ {i}}, \quad g = \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {m} b _ {j} \mathbf {1} _ {B _ {j}},
710: \]
711: 
712: with \(A_{i}, B_{j}\) measurable and \(a_{i}, b_{j} \geq 0\).
713: 
714: We consider the common partition formed by the sets \( C_{i,j} = A_i \cap B_j \), on which:
715: 
716: \[
717: \alpha f + \beta g = \sum_ {i, j} \left(\alpha a _ {i} + \beta b _ {j}\right) \mathbf {1} _ {C _ {i, j}}.
718: \]
719: 
720: The integral becomes:
721: 
722: \[
723: \int_ {E} (\alpha f + \beta g) d \mu = \sum_ {i, j} (\alpha a _ {i} + \beta b _ {j}) \mu (C _ {i, j}) = \alpha \sum_ {i, j} a _ {i} \mu (C _ {i, j}) + \beta \sum_ {i, j} b _ {j} \mu (C _ {i, j}).
724: \]
725: 
726: But:
727: 
728: \[
729: \sum_ {i, j} a _ {i} \mu (C _ {i, j}) = \sum_ {i} a _ {i} \mu (A _ {i}), \quad \sum_ {i, j} b _ {j} \mu (C _ {i, j}) = \sum_ {j} b _ {j} \mu (B _ {j}).
730: \]
731: 
732: Lebesgue Integration
733: 
734: 17
735: 
736: Hence:
737: 
738: $$\int_{E} (\alpha f + \beta g) \, d\mu = \alpha \int_{E} f \, d\mu + \beta \int_{E} g \, d\mu. \quad \square$$
739: 
740: Property 6.6 (Monotonicity). Let $f$ and $g \in \mathcal{E}^+$ functions on a measurable set $E$ such that $f \leq g$ on $E$. Then:
741: 
742: $$\int_{E} f \, d\mu \leq \int_{E} g \, d\mu.$$
743: 
744: Proof. Since $f \leq g$, we have $g - f \geq 0$. Moreover, $g - f$ is still a simple function (being the sum and difference of simple functions).
745: 
746: By positivity of the integral for (non-negative) simple functions:
747: 
748: $$\int_{E} (g - f) \, d\mu \geq 0.$$
749: 
750: Therefore:
751: 
752: $$\int_{E} g \, d\mu - \int_{E} f \, d\mu \geq 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \int_{E} f \, d\mu \leq \int_{E} g \, d\mu. \quad \square$$
753: 
754: Proposition 6.7. Let $f \in \mathcal{E}^+$ function on $X$ and $A \in \mathcal{A}$ a negligible set. Then:
755: 
756: $$\int_{A} f(x) \, dx = 0.$$
757: 
758: Proof. By definition, a simple function $f$ is a linear combination of indicator functions of measurable sets of finite measure:
759: 
760: $$f = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k \mathbf{1}_{E_k},$$
761: 
762: where $a_k \in \mathbb{R}$ and each $E_k$ is measurable with finite measure.
763: 
764: We consider the integral of $f$ over the negligible set $A$:
765: 
766: $$\int_{A} f(x) \, dx = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k \int_{A} \mathbf{1}_{E_k}(x) \, dx.$$
767: 
768: But $\mathbf{1}_{E_k}(x)$ is measurable, and since $A$ is negligible, we have:
769: 
770: $$\int_{A} \mathbf{1}_{E_k}(x) \, dx = \operatorname{mes}(A \cap E_k) = 0.$$
771: 
772: Thus each term of the sum is zero, giving:
773: 
774: $$\int_{A} f(x) \, dx = 0.$$
775: 
776: Proposition 6.8. Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space, and let $f : X \to \mathbb{R}^+$ be a simple function, If
777: 
778: $$\int_{X} f \, d\mu = 0,$$
779: 
780: then $f = 0$ $\mu$-almost everywhere on $X$.
781: 
782: Lebesgue Integration
783: 
784: 18
785: 
786: Proof. A simple function $f$ can be written as $f = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i \mathbf{1}_{A_i}$ with $\lambda_i \geq 0$ and $A_i \in \mathcal{A}$. We have:
787: 
788: $$\int_X f \, d\mu = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i \, \mu(A_i).$$
789: 
790: If this sum is zero, and since all terms are non-negative, for each $i$ we have $\lambda_i \, \mu(A_i) = 0$. Therefore, $\lambda_i = 0$ or $\mu(A_i) = 0$, which implies $f = 0$ almost everywhere.

801:

808:

886: ### 7.3 Definition of the integral
887: 
888: **Definition 7.4** (Lebesgue integral of a non-negative function). Let $(X, \mathscr{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space, and let $f: X \to [0, +\infty]$ be measurable.
889: 
890: The **Lebesgue integral** of $f$ is the value (possibly infinite) defined by:
891: 
892: $$\int_X f \, d\mu := \sup \left\{ \int_X \varphi \, d\mu \ \middle| \ \varphi \text{ is a simple function, } 0 \leq \varphi \leq f \right\}.$$
893: 
894: In particular, if $(\varphi_n)$ is an increasing sequence of simple functions such that $\varphi_n(x) \uparrow f(x)$ for a.e. $x \in X$, then:
895: 
896: $$\int_X f \, d\mu = \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_X \varphi_n \, d\mu.$$
897: 
898: **Remark 7.5.** This definition coincides with that for simple functions: if $f$ is already a non-negative step function, then the supremum is attained for $f$ itself.

987: 
988: - Any nonnegative measurable function can be approximated by an increasing sequence of simple functions.

994: ## 8 Integral of General Functions
995: 
996: Up to this point, we have defined the Lebesgue integral only for positive measurable functions. We will now extend this definition to functions that can take both positive and negative values.
997: 
998: ### 8.1 Positive Part and Negative Part
999: 
1000: Definition 8.1. Let $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$ be a measurable function. We define:
1001: 
1002: $$f^+(x) = \max(f(x), 0), \quad (\text{positive part})$$
1003: 
1004: $$f^-(x) = \max(-f(x), 0), \quad (\text{negative part})$$
1005: 
1006: We then have:
1007: 
1008: $$f = f^+ - f^-, \quad |f| = f^+ + f^-.$$
1009: 
1010: Remark 8.2. The functions $f^+$ and $f^-$ are positive and measurable, since they are obtained through operations that preserve measurability.
1011: 
1012: Lebesgue Integration
1013: 
1014: 23
1015: 
1016: ## 8.2 Definition of the Integral
1017: 
1018: **Definition 8.3** (Lebesgue Integral of a Real Function). *Let $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$ be a measurable function. If the integrals of $f^+$ and $f^-$ are both finite, then we say that $f$ is **integrable**, and we define:*
1019: 
1020: $$\int_E f \, d\mu = \int_E f^+ \, d\mu - \int_E f^- \, d\mu.$$
1021: 
1022: **Remark 8.4.** *The condition $\int f^+ < \infty$ and $\int f^- < \infty$ ensures that the difference is meaningful (not $\infty - \infty$).*
1023: 
1024: **Theorem 8.5.** *A function $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$ is **integrable** (in the sense of Lebesgue) if and only if:*
1025: 
1026: $$\int_X |f| \, d\mu < \infty.$$
1027: 
1028: *Proof.* This follows from the fact that $|f| = f^+ + f^-$ and from Definition 8.3. $\square$
1029: 
1030: ## 8.3 Examples
1031: 
1032: **Example 8.6.** *The function $f(x) = \frac{1}{1+x^2}$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ and decreases rapidly. It is integrable on $\mathbb{R}$:*
1033: 
1034: $$\int_{\mathbb{R}} \frac{1}{1+x^2} \, dx = \pi.$$
1035: 
1036: **Example 8.7.** *The function $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ on $(0, 1]$ is not integrable (in the sense of Lebesgue) because:*
1037: 
1038: $$\int_0^1 \frac{1}{x} \, dx = +\infty.$$
1039: 
1040: ## 8.4 Summary to Remember
1041: 
1042: - Any real measurable function can be decomposed into a positive part $f^+$ and a negative part $f^-$.
1043: - We say that $f$ is integrable if $\int |f| < \infty$.
1044: - The integral of $f$ is then well-defined as the difference between the integrals of $f^+$ and $f^-$.
1045: - This generalizes the notion of algebraic area to very general functions.

1075: 1. \( f_{n}(x) \leq f_{n+1}(x) \) for \( \mu \)-almost every \( x \in X \) and for all \( n \) (pointwise increasing),

1079:

1085: 
1086: \[

1088: \]

1091: 
1092: \[

1094: \]
1095:

1143:

1198: ## 9.4 Lebesgue's Dominated Convergence Theorem
1199: 
1200: Theorem 9.8 (Lebesgue's Dominated Convergence Theorem). Let $(X, \mathscr{A}, \mu)$ be a measure space. Let $(f_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ be a sequence of measurable functions such that:
1201: 
1202: - $f_n(x) \to f(x)$ almost everywhere on $X$;
1203: - there exists a measurable function $g: X \to [0, +\infty]$ such that, for all $n$, $|f_n(x)| \le g(x)$ almost everywhere, and $\int_X g \, d\mu < +\infty$.
1204: 
1205: Then $f$ is integrable, i.e. measurable and $\int_X |f| \, d\mu < +\infty$, and:
1206: 
1207: $$\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_X f_n(x) \, d\mu(x) = \int_X f(x) \, d\mu(x).$$
1208: 
1209: Proof. (1) $f$ is integrable. Since $f_n \to f$ a.e. and the function $x \mapsto |x|$ is continuous, we have $|f_n| \to |f|$ a.e. Moreover, $|f_n| \le g$ a.e. for all $n$, hence by passing to the limit we get $|f| \le g$ a.e. Because $g$ is integrable, it follows that $f \in L^1(\mu)$ and
1210: 
1211: $$\int_X |f| \, d\mu \le \int_X g \, d\mu < \infty.$$
1212: 
1213: Lebesgue Integration
1214: 
1215: 27
1216: 
1217: (2) Fatou-type inequalities to bound the limit superior and limit inferior. Observe that for each $n$, we have $g \pm f_n \geq 0$ a.e. (since $|f_n| \leq g$), and likewise $g \pm f \geq 0$ a.e.
1218: 
1219: First application of Fatou's Lemma. Applying Fatou's Lemma to the nonnegative sequence $(g + f_n)$, we obtain
1220: 
1221: $$\int_X (g + f) \, d\mu \leq \liminf_{n \to \infty} \int_X (g + f_n) \, d\mu.$$
1222:

1224:

1226: 
1227: Subtracting $\int_X g \, d\mu$ from both sides yields
1228:

1235: that is,
1236: 
1237: $$\int_X g \, d\mu - \int_X f \, d\mu \leq \liminf_{n \to \infty} \left( \int_X g \, d\mu - \int_X f_n \, d\mu \right).$$
1238: 
1239: Subtracting $\int_X g \, d\mu$ and changing signs, we deduce
1240: 
1241: $$\limsup_{n \to \infty} \int_X f_n \, d\mu \leq \int_X f \, d\mu.$$
1242: 
1243: (3) Conclusion. From the two previous inequalities,
1244: 
1245: $$\int_X f \, d\mu \leq \liminf_{n \to \infty} \int_X f_n \, d\mu \leq \limsup_{n \to \infty} \int_X f_n \, d\mu \leq \int_X f \, d\mu,$$
1246: 
1247: we conclude that the limit $\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_X f_n \, d\mu$ exists and equals $\int_X f \, d\mu$.
1248: 
1249: Remark 9.9. This proof uses only Fatou's Lemma and the assumption of integrable domination. Note that Step (1) relies solely on the continuity of the absolute value function and the bound $|f_n| \leq g$.

1256: 
1257: $$|f_n(x)| \leq \frac{1}{n} \leq 1 \quad \text{and even } |f_n(x)| \leq |\sin(x)| =: g(x).$$
1258: 
1259: The function $g(x)$ is integrable on $[0, \pi]$, so we can apply the dominated convergence theorem:

1268: 
1269: In this section, we present several concrete examples to illustrate the integrability (or lack thereof) of certain functions, depending on whether we use the Riemann or Lebesgue integral.

1271: ### 10.1 Continuous function: $f(x) = x$
1272: 
1273: Example 10.1. The function $f(x) = x$ on $[0, 1]$ is continuous, so:

1276:

1280:

1284: - It is not Riemann integrable.
1285: - But it is Lebesgue integrable:
1286: 
1287: $$\int_0^1 f(x) \, d\lambda = \lambda(\mathbb{Q} \cap [0, 1]) = 0.$$

1295: - Computation of the integral:
1296: 
1297: $$\int_0^1 \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} \, dx = 2.$$
1298: 
1299: - Therefore: $f$ is Lebesgue integrable (and also Riemann integrable here).

1332: ## 11 $L^p(\Omega)$ Space
1333: 
1334: In this section, we consider $(\Omega, \mathcal{B}, \lambda^N)$ as a measured space, where $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^N$ is an open set, and $\lambda^N$ is the Lebesgue measure on $\Omega$. The notation $d\lambda^N$ will be abbreviated by $dx$. Let $1 \le p \le +\infty$.
1335: 
1336: ### 11.1 Definition and First Properties
1337: 
1338: For $1 \le p < +\infty$, we define:
1339: 
1340: $$\mathcal{L}^p(\Omega) = \left\{ f : \Omega \to \mathbb{R} \text{ (or } \mathbb{C} \text{), measurable, } \int_\Omega |f(x)|^p \, dx < +\infty \right\}.$$
1341: 
1342: For $p = +\infty$, we define:
1343: 
1344: $$\mathcal{L}^\infty(\Omega) = \{ f : \Omega \to \mathbb{R} \text{ (or } \mathbb{C} \text{), measurable, } \exists C > 0 \text{ such that } |f(x)| \le C \text{ a.e. on } \Omega \}.$$
1345: 
1346: Associated Norms. For $f \in \mathcal{L}^p(\Omega)$, we define:
1347: 
1348: - If $1 \le p < +\infty$, then $\|f\|_p = \left( \int_\Omega |f(x)|^p \, dx \right)^{1/p}$.
1349: - If $p = +\infty$, then $\|f\|_\infty = \operatorname{esssup}_{x \in \Omega} |f(x)| = \inf \{ C > 0 \mid |f(x)| \le C \text{ a.e. on } \Omega \}$.

1351: Proposition 11.1. $\|\cdot\|_p$ is a seminorm on $\mathcal{L}^p(\Omega)$.
1352: 
1353: Proof. Positivity and homogeneity are straightforward. The triangle inequality follows from Minkowski's inequality (see Chapter 1). It is not a norm on $\mathcal{L}^p(\Omega)$ because if $\|f\|_p = \left( \int_\Omega |f(x)|^p \, dx \right)^{1/p} = 0$, this implies that $f(x) = 0$ a.e. on $\Omega$ and not everywhere, so $f$ is not necessarily the zero function. $\square$

The fact that  \( \|f\|_{p}=0 \)  does not necessarily imply f=0 everywhere, but only f=0 almost everywhere, prevents having a norm on  \( \mathcal{L}^{p}(\Omega) \) . To address this, we define an equivalence relation  \( f\sim g \)  if f=g almost everywhere on  \( \Omega \) . The quotient space is:

1396:

1398:

1406: 
1407: \[
1408: a b \leq \frac {1}{p} a ^ {p} + \frac {1}{p ^ {\prime}} b ^ {p ^ {\prime}},
1409: \]

1411: with equality if \(a^p = b^{p'}\).

1413: Lebesgue Integration

1543: ## 13 Product Measures and Tonelli and Fubini Theorems
1544: 
1545: When working with functions defined on a product of measured spaces, it is natural to want to define a multiple integral. For this, we must introduce the notion of a product measure and use the Tonelli and Fubini theorems.
1546: 
1547: ### 13.1 Product Measures
1548: 
1549: **Definition 13.1** (Product $\sigma$-algebra). *Let $(X, \mathcal{A})$ and $(Y, \mathcal{B})$ be two measurable spaces.*
1550: 
1551: *The product $\sigma$-algebra on $X \times Y$ is the set $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$, the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing all measurable rectangles of the form $A \times B$, with $A \in \mathcal{A}$ and $B \in \mathcal{B}$.*
1552: 
1553: *In other words:*
1554: 
1555: $$\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B} = \sigma \left( \{ A \times B \mid A \in \mathcal{A}, B \in \mathcal{B} \} \right),$$
1556: 
1557: *where $\sigma(\cdot)$ denotes the generated $\sigma$-algebra.*
1558: 
1559: **Remark 13.2.** *The sets $A \times B$ are called measurable rectangles and form the basic building blocks of the construction.*
1560: 
1561: **Definition 13.3** (Product Measure). *Let $(X, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ and $(Y, \mathcal{B}, \nu)$ be two measured spaces. There exists a unique measure $\mu \otimes \nu$ on $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$ such that:*
1562: 
1563: $$(\mu \otimes \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A) \cdot \nu(B), \quad \text{for all } A \in \mathcal{A}, \ B \in \mathcal{B}.$$
1564: 
1565: *This defines the product measured space $(X \times Y, \mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}, \mu \otimes \nu)$.*
1566: 
1567: **Example 13.4** (Lebesgue Product Measure). *Let*
1568: 
1569: $$\Omega_1 \subset \mathbb{R}^{N_1}, \quad \Omega_2 \subset \mathbb{R}^{N_2}$$
1570: 
1571: *be two measurable sets. We endow $\Omega_1$ with the Lebesgue measure $m_{N_1}$ (denoted $dx$) and $\Omega_2$ with the Lebesgue measure $m_{N_2}$ (denoted $dy$).*
1572: 
1573: *The product measure*
1574: 
1575: $$m_{N_1} \times m_{N_2}$$
1576: 
1577: *is defined on the measurable product $\Omega_1 \times \Omega_2 \subset \mathbb{R}^{N_1+N_2}$ by:*
1578: 
1579: $$(m_{N_1} \times m_{N_2})(A) = \int_{\Omega_1} \left( \int_{\Omega_2} \mathbf{1}_A(x, y) \, dy \right) dx,$$
1580: 
1581: *for any measurable set $A \subset \Omega_1 \times \Omega_2$.*
1582: 
1583: *In particular, if $A = A_1 \times A_2$ with $A_1 \subset \Omega_1$ and $A_2 \subset \Omega_2$ measurable, we have:*
1584: 
1585: $$(m_{N_1} \times m_{N_2})(A_1 \times A_2) = m_{N_1}(A_1) m_{N_2}(A_2).$$
1586: 
1587: *This measure corresponds exactly to the Lebesgue measure $m_{N_1+N_2}$ restricted to $\Omega_1 \times \Omega_2$.*

1593: ### 13.2 Tonelli's Theorem (case of positive functions)
1594: 
1595: Theorem 13.5 (Tonelli). Let \( f: X \times Y \to [0, +\infty] \) be a measurable function (on the product \( \sigma \)-algebra). Then:
1596: 
1597: \[
1598: \int_ {X \times Y} f (x, y) d (\mu \otimes \nu) (x, y) = \int_ {X} \left(\int_ {Y} f (x, y) d \nu (y)\right) d \mu (x) = \int_ {Y} \left(\int_ {X} f (x, y) d \mu (x)\right) d \nu (y).
1599: \]
1600: 
1601: In other words, we can interchange the integrals, even if the total integral is infinite.
1602: 
1603: Idea of the proof. The idea is to construct an increasing sequence of simple functions \((\varphi_{n})\) such that \(\varphi_{n} \uparrow f\), and to use:
1604: 
1605: - the definition of the Lebesgue integral via approximations;
1606: • the Beppo-Levi theorem;
1607: - the equality of iterated integrals for simple functions.
1608: 
1609: Indeed, for a positive simple function:
1610: 
1611: \[
1612: \varphi (x, y) = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n} a _ {k} \cdot \chi_ {A _ {k} \times B _ {k}} (x, y),
1613: \]
1614: 
1615: we have:
1616: 
1617: \[
1618: \int_ {X \times Y} \varphi d (\mu \otimes \nu) = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n} a _ {k} \mu (A _ {k}) \nu (B _ {k}).
1619: \]
1620: 
1621: By the definition of the iterated integral, we can also write:
1622: 
1623: \[
1624: \int_ {X} \left(\int_ {Y} \varphi (x, y) d \nu (y)\right) d \mu (x) = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n} a _ {k} \mu (A _ {k}) \nu (B _ {k}).
1625: \]
1626: 
1627: Thus, the equality holds for simple functions and extends to positive measurable functions by taking the limit (Beppo-Levi).

1649: Theorem 13.7 (Fubini). Let \( f: X \times Y \to \mathbb{R} \) be a measurable function such that \( f \in L^{1}(X \times Y, \mu \otimes \nu) \). Then:
1650: 
1651: - For almost every \( x \), the function \( y \mapsto f(x, y) \) is integrable over \( Y \), and the function \( x \mapsto \int_{Y} f(x, y) d\nu(y) \) is integrable over \( X \); the same holds symmetrically when exchanging \( x \) and \( y \).
1652: - We have:
1653: 
1654: \[
1655: \int_ {X \times Y} f (x, y) d (\mu \otimes \nu) (x, y) = \int_ {X} \left(\int_ {Y} f (x, y) d \nu (y)\right) d \mu (x) = \int_ {Y} \left(\int_ {X} f (x, y) d \mu (x)\right) d \nu (y)
1656: \]
1657: 
1658: Idea of the proof. We apply Tonelli's theorem to \( |f| \), which is positive and integrable by hypothesis. This ensures that the absolute integral can be computed via iterated integrals.
1659: 
1660: Then, we use the linearity of the integral and the fact that the integrals of \( f^{+} \) and \( f^{-} \) (the positive and negative parts of \( f \)) are finite, to extend the result to \( f \).
1661: 
1662: The complete proof treats the positive/negative cases separately, but the essence is: **Fubini = Tonelli + integrability**.
1663: 
1664: Example 13.8. Let \( f(x,y) = x \cdot y \) on \([0,1]^2\). Then \( f \) is continuous, hence integrable.
1665: 
1666: We compute:
1667: 
1668: \[
1669: \int_ {0} ^ {1} \int_ {0} ^ {1} x y d y d x = \int_ {0} ^ {1} \left[ \frac {1}{2} x \right] d x = \frac {1}{4}.
1670: \]
1671: 
1672: Fubini guarantees that the order of integration can be exchanged:
1673: 
1674: \[
1675: \int_ {0} ^ {1} \int_ {0} ^ {1} x y d x d y = \int_ {0} ^ {1} \left[ \frac {1}{2} y \right] d y = \frac {1}{4}.
1676: \]

1678: ### 13.4 Counterexample to Fubini: non-integrable function
1679: 
1680: Example 13.9 (Counterexample to Fubini). Let \( f(x,y) = \frac{1}{x + y} \) on \( X = Y = (0,1) \), with Lebesgue measure.
1681: 
1682: This function is positive, but:
1683: 
1684: \[
1685: \int_ {(0, 1) ^ {2}} f (x, y) d x d y = \int_ {0} ^ {1} \int_ {0} ^ {1} \frac {1}{x + y} d x d y = + \infty .
1686: \]
1687: 
1688: Thus \( f \) is not in \( L^1((0,1)^2) \).

1691: 
1692: \[

1873: 
1874: The Lebesgue integral was designed to overcome the limitations of the Riemann integral. Although the two coincide for many usual functions, they are based on very different perspectives on integration.

1877:

1880: 
1881: In other words: - Riemann partitions the **domain** ($x$-axis), - Lebesgue partitions the **codomain** ($y$-axis).
